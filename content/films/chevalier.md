Six men on a luxury yacht in the Aegean, at the end of a fishing and diving trip, get bored one evening and agree to a game. Whoever is judged "best in general" by the end of the voyage wins the chevalier, a signet ring. From then on everything becomes a metric: sleep quality, cholesterol levels, phone manner, how they clean the bathroom, how they assemble flat-pack furniture, erection firmness. They take notes on each other. Nobody laughs about it for long.

Athina Rachel Tsangari, who made Attenberg and produced Dogtooth, belongs to the generation that put Greek cinema back on the festival map. Chevalier is her absurdist masterpiece, and the funniest, most precise film about masculinity I have seen. It doesn't argue a thesis. It simply observes, with anthropological patience, how men behave when status is the only currency. The deadpan framing does half the work. The other half belongs to a superb ensemble, who play grown men regressing into schoolboys without ever winking at the camera.

There are no women on board, and none are needed: the film is about what men perform for each other. It won Best Film at the BFI London Film Festival and at the Hellenic Film Academy Awards. The Aegean has never looked more beautiful or more ridiculous. Essential Greek cinema, and a mirror many men will prefer not to look into.

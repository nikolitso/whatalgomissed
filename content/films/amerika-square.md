Athens, at the height of the Greek crisis. Amerika Square, once a respectable middle-class neighbourhood, has become home to thousands of migrants and refugees. Nakos, unemployed and living with his parents, watches the new arrivals from his balcony and logs them like an amateur border guard, nursing a resentment that is turning into something worse. His old friend Billy, a tattoo artist, has fallen for Tereza, an African singer trying to make a life in the city. And Tarek, a Syrian refugee, is trying to find a way out of Greece and through its bureaucratic and criminal maze.

Yannis Sakaridis refuses easy villains and easy heroes. The nationalist is pathetic before he is dangerous. The refugees are people before they are symbols. Makis Papadimitriou is excellent as Nakos: funny, sad and frightening, a man who blames others because blaming himself would be too much. The three stories cross in the streets, cafés and apartment blocks around the square, and the film moves between them with real control.

The square is real, and Sakaridis captures the place where the crisis, economic and humanitarian, was most visible in the city. It is a portrait of an Athens that had run out of patience for everyone, including itself. It won Best Film at Thessaloniki, and it remains one of the few Greek films of the period to look straight at the migration crisis without slogans. Uncomfortable, humane, and very Athenian.

Jacob's sister dies on her wedding night. The official explanation is suicide, and nobody around her seems inclined to question it. Jacob does. He travels to the grey, half-empty place where she lived her last months, takes up with her widower, and starts asking questions that nobody wants to answer. What he finds disrupts everything he thought he knew about his sister, his family and himself.

Jannik Johansen, who had made the caper comedy Stealing Rembrandt, goes somewhere much darker here. Nikolaj Lie Kaas plays Jacob as a man whose grief is turning into obsession, and you are never quite sure whether to trust his instincts or fear them. The film moves slowly and deliberately. Information arrives in fragments. The landscape is flat, wet and wintry, and the people in it are polite in the way that hides things.

It is a psychological thriller that operates through atmosphere as much as plot: cold, grey and deeply unsettling in the way only Scandinavian crime cinema manages when it's working properly. There are no big set pieces, no detectives with tragic backstories, just a brother who cannot let go. It arrived a few years before the Nordic noir boom on television and was largely ignored outside Denmark, which is a shame. It deserves more attention. Watch it on a dark evening, alone.

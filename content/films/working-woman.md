Orna is a mother of three in Tel Aviv. Her husband is struggling to get his new restaurant off the ground, and the family needs money. So she is thrilled to land a job at a real estate firm, working for Benny, a charming former army officer who is now building luxury apartments by the sea. She turns out to be very good at selling them. Benny notices. And slowly, then not so slowly, the attention turns into something else.

Precise and uncomfortably real. Michal Aviad, who started out as a documentary filmmaker, shows harassment not as one big scene but as a pattern: a compliment, a touch, an apology, a promotion, a moment alone. Each step is small enough to excuse, and each one makes it harder to leave. The film is very good on the trap of money, on why Orna can't just walk out, and on her shame at telling her husband, who loves her but might not understand.

Liron Ben-Shlush delivers one of Israeli cinema's great recent performances. Almost the whole film rests on her face: her pride in her work, her growing fear, her careful calculations. Menashe Noy makes Benny frightening precisely because he isn't a monster, just a powerful man used to getting his way. The film premiered at Toronto in 2018, and arrived right in the middle of the #MeToo moment, but it was developed before it. It doesn't need the headlines. A quiet, controlled film that leaves you angry.

A rifle changes hands in the Moroccan desert. Two boys herding goats test it, and a bullet hits an American tourist, Susan, on a bus, travelling with her husband Richard. Their children are back in California with their Mexican nanny, who decides to take them to her son's wedding across the border. In Tokyo, a deaf teenager, isolated and angry after her mother's death, searches for connection in a city that can't hear her. Four stories, four countries, one moment of carelessness.

This was the third film of Iñárritu's collaboration with writer Guillermo Arriaga, after Amores Perros and 21 Grams, and by far the most ambitious. It is about language, borders and how easily people who cannot understand each other turn into threats. Brad Pitt and Cate Blanchett are the famous names, but the film belongs to the others: Adriana Barraza as the nanny, and Rinko Kikuchi as the Tokyo girl, both nominated for Oscars. The Japanese story is the best of the four, almost wordless, and the least connected to the plot.

It is uneven and occasionally overwrought. The coincidences are heavy and the misery sometimes feels engineered. But the scale is impressive and the craft is undeniable. Iñárritu won Best Director at Cannes, the film was nominated for Best Picture, and Gustavo Santaolalla won the Oscar for his score. Still one of the most formally daring things Hollywood financed that decade.

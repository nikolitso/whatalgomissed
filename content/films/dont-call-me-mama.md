Nina Knag’s *Don’t Call Me Mama* takes an initially familiar premise — a middle-aged woman becoming involved with a young asylum seeker — and turns it into something considerably more uncomfortable and unpredictable. What begins as a story about compassion, loneliness and integration gradually mutates into a tense psychosexual thriller about desire, power and self-interest.

Eva, played with considerable vulnerability and volatility by Pia Tjelta, is a volunteer teacher helping asylum seekers navigate the Norwegian system. Her marriage to Jonstein, a politically ambitious mayor played by Kristoffer Joner, has effectively collapsed, leaving her emotionally and sexually frustrated. When she develops a fascination with Amir, an intelligent 18-year-old Syrian refugee, the film deliberately refuses to offer an easy explanation for what follows. Is Eva trying to save him, replace something missing in her marriage, or simply satisfy a desire she has been suppressing?

The answer, increasingly, is all three.

What makes the film particularly effective is the way it connects Eva’s personal desires with the political hypocrisy surrounding Amir. Jonstein is perfectly happy to welcome the young refugee into his home when doing so can benefit his campaign, turning hospitality into political capital. The supposedly altruistic family gesture therefore becomes another transaction. Everyone has an agenda, even when they pretend otherwise.

This is where *Don’t Call Me Mama* becomes more interesting than a conventional forbidden-romance thriller. Knag uses the relationship to examine the uncomfortable gap between **what people claim to do for others and what they actually want for themselves**. Immigration, marriage, politics, sex and family become different arenas in which the characters negotiate their own interests.

The film's greatest strength is that it doesn't divide its characters neatly into victims and predators, saints and villains. Eva is both sympathetic and deeply troubling. Jonstein's political opportunism is obvious, but his private compromises make him more complicated. Amir, meanwhile, is never simply the innocent refugee around whom the Norwegian characters project their anxieties and desires. Each character is capable of manipulation, deception and self-preservation.

That moral instability gives the film its tension. The closer Eva and Amir become, the more the film shifts away from social drama and towards psychological thriller, with Knag steadily increasing the sense that something is going to go badly wrong. The sexual element is therefore less about eroticism than about **power, secrecy and the consequences of crossing boundaries**.

There are moments when the film pushes its symbolism a little too clearly, and some of its sociopolitical observations are hardly subtle. But the combination of an increasingly dysfunctional marriage, political calculation and an unpredictable central relationship keeps the story engaging. Pia Tjelta and Kristoffer Joner are particularly strong, giving their characters enough contradictions to prevent the film from becoming a simple morality play.

Ultimately, *Don’t Call Me Mama* is less interested in asking whether its characters are good or bad than in asking **what they are willing to sacrifice to get what they want**. Compassion can become manipulation. Hospitality can become political theatre. Marriage can become a negotiation. Desire can become destructive.

Knag's debut is therefore an unexpectedly sharp blend of social drama, dark comedy and psychosexual thriller. It leaves you with an uncomfortable idea: perhaps the most dangerous thing about helping someone is discovering how much you expect in return.

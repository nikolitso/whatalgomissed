Thomas is a businessman drowning in debt, cornered by a loan shark, with days left to save his home. You would expect a man in that position to change course. He doesn't. He doubles down on his vices, surrounds himself with enablers who tell him what he wants to hear, and lives under the sincere conviction that his struggle is heroic.

Economides has been mapping this territory since Matchbox and Knifer: Greek men trapped in rooms, in families, in their own tempers. Broken Vein is his most politically charged film, set in the long shadow of the debt crisis, but it is really a portrait of a specific Greek masculine pathology. This is the man who mistakes stubbornness for dignity and self-destruction for resistance. Every bad decision is presented to himself as a stand on principle. The camera stays close, the dialogue is loud and profane, and the humour is the kind that sticks in your throat.

What makes it work is that Economides never turns Thomas into a symbol. He is too specific for that, too petty and too human, and you recognise him from somewhere: a relative, a neighbour, a man at the next table. It plays like a modern tragedy rooted in hubris, with the chorus replaced by creditors and hangers-on. The crisis didn't create Thomas. It just removed the structures that were keeping him functional. Not comfortable viewing, but the most honest Greek film about the crisis years I have seen.

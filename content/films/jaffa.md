In Jaffa, the old mixed Arab-Jewish city next to Tel Aviv, Reuven runs a car repair garage with his family. His daughter Mali works in the office. His son Meir works on the cars, badly, and resents everyone. And Toufik, a young Palestinian mechanic, works alongside them. Mali and Toufik have been secretly in love for years, and they are planning to run away together. Then a tragedy permanently changes the course of both their lives.

Keren Yedaya, who won the Caméra d'Or at Cannes with her debut Or, works with long, patient scenes in confined spaces: the garage, the family kitchen, a small flat. She is interested less in the conflict than in the suffocating weight of family, gender and what young people sacrifice to keep the peace at home. Politics is present in every glance and every silence, but nobody gives speeches. Dana Ivgy is quietly devastating as Mali, and the late Ronit Elkabetz, as her mother, can hold a scene with a single look.

The film is slow, and some will find its restraint frustrating. But the restraint is the point. These are people who have learned not to say the important things, and the cost of that silence builds and builds. A sad, intimate film about a love that the city itself seems designed to make impossible.

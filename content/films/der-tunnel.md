Berlin, August 1961. The Wall goes up almost overnight. Harry Melchior, a champion swimmer in the East, escapes to the West, but his sister and her family are left behind. Harry gathers a group of friends, students and fellow refugees, each with someone on the other side, and they start digging a tunnel under the border from the Western side.

Roland Suso Richter, working from real escape stories of the early 1960s, builds it as a slow-burning thriller. The physical work is exhausting and claustrophobic: wet clay, flooding, a few metres a day. Above ground the danger is different. The Stasi is watching, there may be an informer in the group, and every person who learns the plan puts everyone else at risk. Heino Ferch gives Harry a hard edge, a man whose determination is admirable and sometimes frightening.

It plays like a big-screen epic, and it is tense, human and largely forgotten outside Germany. That is a shame, because it is one of the great Cold War dramas: precise in its suspense and honest about what freedom actually costs. Not just effort, but the people around you who bear the risk. Long, at well over two and a half hours, but it never drags. Find it.

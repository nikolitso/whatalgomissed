After his father dies, Nikos leaves the provinces for the western edges of Athens, where his uncle has offered him work guarding the dogs he breeds. The uncle is a brute who rules his house by fear. His wife is younger, bored and watching. When she starts to draw closer to Nikos, the balance of power in the house begins to shift, and what follows is a vicious circle of envy, lust and the struggle for dominance in a household held together by resentment.

Economides shoots it in black and white, and the choice is not decorative. It drains the city of colour and warmth, leaving concrete, wire fences and faces. The dialogue is sparse where Matchbox was a wall of noise. People say very little, and what they say is usually a threat or a lie. Nikos is passive at first, almost blank, and the film's real subject is what this world does to a young man who arrives with nothing and learns, quickly, how things work here.

This is his most formally controlled film. Where Matchbox screams, Knifer suffocates. The violence, when it comes, feels inevitable rather than shocking, as if the film has been counting down to it from the first frame. It swept the Hellenic Film Academy Awards with seven wins, including Best Film, Best Director and Best Screenplay, and confirmed Economides as the coldest, most honest observer of Greek life of his generation. Not a comfortable evening, but a very good one.

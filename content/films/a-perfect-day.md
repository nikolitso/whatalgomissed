A single day in Rome. Antonio, a bodyguard for a politician, cannot accept that his wife Emma has left him and taken their two children. She is trying to start again with very little money and a lot of fear. Around them, other Romans drift through their own quiet crises, including the politician Antonio protects and the people closest to him. The title is the first of many ironies.

Ferzan Özpetek, the Turkish-born director who has spent his career making Italian films, is usually a filmmaker of warmth and big, noisy tables. Here he goes darker. Adapted from a novel by Melania Mazzucco, the film moves between its characters over twenty-four hours, and the tension builds without anyone raising their voice. Valerio Mastandrea is frightening as Antonio, a man whose love has turned into something possessive and dangerous. Isabella Ferrari gives Emma a tired, fragile courage.

It is quiet, observational, and deeply sad in the way only Italian domestic realism manages: the kind of sadness that comes from recognising that the catastrophe has already happened and everyone is just living inside it. Not everything works. Some of the side stories feel thinner than the central one. But the main thread is gripping and the final hours are hard to shake. It competed at Venice in 2008. A film to watch when you are ready for it.

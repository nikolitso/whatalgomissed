Accio and Manrico are brothers from a working-class family in Latina, the town Mussolini built on the drained marshes south of Rome. Manrico is handsome, charismatic and a Communist. Accio, the younger, is angry, clever and contrary, and mostly to spite everyone he joins the local fascists. Across the 1960s and 70s, through strikes, occupations and street fights, the two brothers find themselves on opposing political extremes, and in love with the same woman, yet remain bound by something stronger than ideology.

Daniele Luchetti adapts Antonio Pennacchi's novel with a light touch and a lot of energy. The script, co-written with Sandro Petraglia and Stefano Rulli, never lectures. Politics here is personal, chaotic and often ridiculous: Accio drifts in and out of movements as much out of loneliness as conviction. The family scenes, crammed into a small flat, are some of the funniest and truest in modern Italian cinema.

Funny, furious and genuinely moving. Elio Germano is extraordinary as Accio, a man who can't stop picking the wrong fight for the right reasons, and Riccardo Scamarcio is better than he usually gets credit for as Manrico. It played in Un Certain Regard at Cannes and was the start of a strong partnership between Luchetti and Germano that continued with Our Life. One of the best Italian films about that era, and certainly the warmest.

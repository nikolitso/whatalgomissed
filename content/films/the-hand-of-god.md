Naples, 1984. Teenage Fabietto lives inside a large, eccentric, loving family: parents who play pranks on each other, aunts and uncles who gather for long lunches by the sea, a beautiful aunt whose troubles fascinate him. And Diego Maradona has just arrived at Napoli, which in this city is a religious event. Then tragedy strikes, and the connection to Maradona becomes something more than sport.

This is Sorrentino's most personal film, part Fellini homage and part raw autobiography about the loss that made him a filmmaker. He tells it with less of his usual baroque camera and more patience. The first half glows: the family scenes are crowded, funny and warm, the kind of chaos you only miss once it's gone. The second breaks something in you. Fabietto drifts, grieves, and slowly finds his way towards cinema, pushed along by a fiery local director, Antonio Capuano, who tells him to stop whining and find something to say.

Filippo Scotti, playing Sorrentino's young self, won the Marcello Mastroianni award for best young actor at Venice, and Toni Servillo and Teresa Saponangelo are wonderful as the parents. The film won the Grand Jury Prize at Venice and was nominated for the Oscar. If The Great Beauty was Sorrentino looking at Rome from a distance, this is him finally looking at himself. His best film.

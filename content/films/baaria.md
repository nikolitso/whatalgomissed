Tornatore goes home. Baarìa is the Sicilian name for Bagheria, the town near Palermo where he was born, and this is his sprawling autobiographical epic about it, spanning three generations from the 1930s to the 1980s. At its centre is Peppino, the son of a poor peasant family, who grows up through fascism, war and poverty, becomes a committed Communist, and marries Mannina against her family's wishes. Around them, the whole town lives, argues, sings and grows old.

The film is ambitious and overflowing with life. Dozens of characters pass through the square and the main street, and the decades change around them: politics, cinema, emigration, the arrival of the modern world. Francesco Scianna and Margareth Madè are appealing as the couple, and the big crowd scenes have real energy. It is unmistakably personal, full of childhood memory and the small myths every family tells about itself.

It is not his most controlled film. The structure is loose, the pace sometimes rushed, and some episodes are gone before they have registered. But it is possibly his most felt, a film by a man who loves the place he comes from and is honest enough to show its poverty and its violence too. It opened the Venice Film Festival in 2009. Ennio Morricone's score is among his finest late-career work. If you loved Cinema Paradiso, this is its bigger, messier, generous cousin.

Simin wants to leave Iran with her husband Nader and their eleven-year-old daughter Termeh, for a better life abroad. Nader refuses. He won't abandon his father, who has Alzheimer's. The film opens with the two of them facing a family judge, arguing straight at the camera, and from that first scene we are asked to judge too. When Simin moves out, Nader hires Razieh, a poor and deeply religious woman, to care for his father. He doesn't know she is pregnant.

What begins as a domestic dispute becomes a collision of class, faith, pride and moral ambiguity that implicates everyone in the room. An incident on the stairs, a missing sum of money, a lie told to protect someone: Farhadi builds the case piece by piece, and every time you think you know who is right, the ground moves. Leila Hatami, Peyman Moaadi, Sareh Bayat and Shahab Hosseini are all superb, and the daughter, watching the adults fail, may be the real centre of the film.

Farhadi refuses to assign blame, and that is his genius. A Separation won the Golden Bear at Berlin, where the cast also shared both acting prizes, and it became the first Iranian film to win the Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film. It deserved every award. One of the greatest films of the 21st century, and the most precise portrait of a society under pressure I have seen.

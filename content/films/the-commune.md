Copenhagen, the 1970s. Erik, an architecture lecturer, inherits his father's huge house. It is too big for him, his wife Anna, a television newsreader, and their teenage daughter Freja, so Anna proposes that they fill it with friends and turn it into a commune. The 1970s idealism feels genuine: the warmth of communal living, the collective meals, the house meetings where everything is voted on, the beer and the singing.

Then Erik falls for one of his young students, Emma, and Anna, trying to be modern, suggests she move in. Everything begins to collapse. It is Vinterberg's most personal film, drawn from his own childhood growing up in a commune, and adapted from his own stage play. He looks back at that world with affection and a bit of irony, and he never mocks the dream. He simply shows what happens when an ideal meets ordinary jealousy, and how the children watch the adults fail. Ulrich Thomsen is excellent as Erik, a man who preaches freedom until it costs him something.

Trine Dyrholm won Best Actress at Berlin for what is arguably the most demanding performance in her extraordinary career. Anna's slow collapse, smiling through her own humiliation, is painful to watch and impossible to look away from. The film is not as perfect as The Celebration or The Hunt, but it may be his warmest and saddest. A beautiful, bittersweet film about the limits of our good intentions.

The true story of Tommaso Buscetta, the first major Mafia boss to break omertà and testify against Cosa Nostra. It opens at a party in Palermo in the early eighties, all the families pretending to be at peace, and then the killing starts. Buscetta is in Brazil when the Corleonesi wipe out his allies and members of his own family. Arrested and sent back to Italy, he decides to talk, and the man he talks to is the judge Giovanni Falcone.

Bellocchio directs with operatic intensity. The centre of the film is the Maxi Trial in Palermo, hundreds of defendants shouting from cages, lawyers performing, mafiosi heckling, stripping off their shirts, playing to the gallery. It plays like a grotesque opera, and at the same time like the most serious kind of courtroom drama. Pierfrancesco Favino disappears completely into the role. He makes a man whose choices are morally ambiguous completely comprehensible: vain, brave, a killer who insists he is the last man of honour, and possibly the only one who believed in the old code.

Bellocchio was almost eighty when he made it, more than fifty years after Fists in the Pocket, and he directs like a young man. The film played in competition at Cannes and won six David di Donatello awards, including Best Film and Best Actor for Favino. It is long and dense, and you need some patience for the names. But it is the best Italian film about the Mafia in years.

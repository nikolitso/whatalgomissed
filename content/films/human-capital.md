Christmas Eve in the Brianza, the wealthy hinterland north of Milan. A waiter cycling home from a party is hit by an SUV, and the driver drives on. The accident connects three families across class lines. Dino, an estate agent desperate to climb, has borrowed money he doesn't have to invest in the hedge fund of Giovanni Bernaschi, whose son is dating Dino's daughter. Bernaschi's wife Carla, bored and lonely in a mansion, wants to save a local theatre.

Paolo Virzì, adapting a novel by Stephen Amidon set in Connecticut, transplants it perfectly to Lombardy. The film is told in chapters, from multiple perspectives, each going back over the same days and revealing something the others concealed. It works as a sharp, well-constructed thriller, but its real subject is class complicity and financial speculation: the way money turns everything, people included, into something with a price.

The cast is excellent. Fabrizio Bentivoglio makes Dino both funny and contemptible, and Valeria Bruni Tedeschi is heartbreaking as Carla. The title comes from the insurance industry: the formula used to calculate what a human life is worth. By the end, you know exactly what the film thinks of that formula. This is Virzì's most formally controlled film, and it won the David di Donatello for Best Film. A cold, elegant portrait of Italy's rich and the people who serve them.

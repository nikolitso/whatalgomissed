Nira is a kindergarten teacher in Tel Aviv, in her forties, married, a little bored, attending an evening poetry workshop where nobody takes her very seriously. One day she hears Yoav, a five-year-old in her class, pacing up and down and reciting a poem that seems to come from nowhere. It is startlingly good. She becomes transfixed by his gift, starts writing down everything he says, and begins passing his poems off as her own.

Nadav Lapid's disquieting film is about obsession, cultural anxiety, and what happens when a person mistakes possession for nurturing. Nira's conviction is that poetry is dying in a country obsessed with money, the army and noise, and that this child must be protected from a world that will crush him. She may even be right. But her protection becomes something else, and Lapid films her descent with a strange, restless camera that never lets you settle into either sympathy or judgement. Lapid has said that he wrote poems as a small boy himself, and the film feels like it comes from somewhere personal.

Sarit Larry is fearless as Nira, and the young Avi Shnaidman is uncanny as Yoav, a child who seems to channel his poems more than write them. The film premiered in the Critics' Week at Cannes and later got an American remake with Maggie Gyllenhaal, which is decent but much tamer. Start with the original. Lapid would go on to win the Golden Bear with Synonyms, but this is where his anger first finds its full shape.

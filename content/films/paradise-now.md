Said and Khaled are childhood friends in Nablus. They fix cars, smoke, drink tea on a hill overlooking the city, and wait. One day they are told they have been chosen for a suicide bombing in Tel Aviv, to take place the next day. They accept. The film follows them through the last hours: the farewell video, the shaving, the suit, the crossing. And then things don't go as planned.

Hany Abu-Assad refuses to make them monsters. He makes them human, which is the more unsettling choice. Kais Nashif and Ali Suliman play the two men as ordinary, a little lost, sometimes even funny. The organisation behind them is bureaucratic and almost absurd: the camera jams during the martyrdom video. And there is Suha, played by Lubna Azabal, a young woman back from abroad who argues against violence and gives the film its counterweight.

It is a film that asks what pushes ordinary people toward the unthinkable, without ever excusing the answer. Abu-Assad gives the arguments space, and doesn't pretend to resolve them. Shot in Nablus under very difficult conditions, it won the Golden Globe for Best Foreign Language Film and became the first Palestinian film nominated for an Oscar. It was controversial then and it still is, which is to its credit. Brave, tense and uncomfortable in the right way.

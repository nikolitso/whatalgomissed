Two brothers meet for dinner in Rome with their wives, as they do every month. Massimo is a successful, cynical lawyer who defends anyone who pays. Paolo is a paediatric surgeon, a man of principles and liberal opinions. They don't much like each other. Then a security camera shows a brutal attack on a homeless woman, and the parents recognise the two teenagers on the footage: Massimo's daughter and Paolo's son. Now the question is what to do.

Adapted from Herman Koch's novel The Dinner, the film is set mostly around tables, in restaurants and kitchens, and De Matteo turns each meal into a moral battleground. The comfortable liberal values of the professional classes are tested against the instinct to protect their own, and nobody reacts the way you expect. Alessandro Gassman, Luigi Lo Cascio, Giovanna Mezzogiorno and Barbara Bobulova are all excellent, and the shifts in who stands where are the real drama.

The kids themselves are almost the most frightening thing in it: polite, bored, glued to their phones, quite unable to understand what the fuss is about. De Matteo never explains them, and he doesn't need to. The questions land on the parents, and on us. Better than the other adaptations of the same book, and much more Italian in its view of family as the one law above all others. One of the sharpest Italian films about class and complicity in recent memory. It will start arguments.

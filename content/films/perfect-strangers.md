Seven old friends gather for dinner at a Rome apartment on the night of a lunar eclipse. Three couples and one man whose new girlfriend has, conveniently, stayed at home. Someone suggests a game: every phone goes on the table, every call goes on speaker, every message is read aloud. Nobody can refuse without looking guilty, so nobody refuses.

What follows is a deceptively simple premise that becomes a razor-sharp dissection of modern intimacy, trust, and the things we hide from the people we're supposed to be closest to. Genovese keeps it almost entirely in one room, and he directs it like a stage farce that slowly loses its smile. The laughs come first. Then a message lands that nobody can laugh off, and the jokes start to sound like confessions. The cast, including Marco Giallini, Valerio Mastandrea, Alba Rohrwacher and Kasia Smutniak, is a who's who of contemporary Italian acting, and every one of them gets a moment where the mask slips.

It won Best Film at the David di Donatello, and it holds the Guinness World Record as the most remade film in history, with versions from Spain and France to Greece and Korea. None of them beat the original. Few films understand so well that the phone in your pocket is the most private room you own. Watch it with your partner, then decide whether to unlock your screen.

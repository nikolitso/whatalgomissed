The Rheinwalds run one of Copenhagen's oldest bakeries, a family business passed down through generations and proud of it. Ditte, the daughter, has finally built a life of her own: a gallery, a boyfriend, and a job offer in New York. Then her father Rikard, the charismatic patriarch who has always been at the center of everything, learns that he is terminally ill. And he wants Ditte to take over.

What follows is not a hospital drama but a film about what happens to a family when its center collapses, and who ends up carrying the weight nobody admits is there. Rikard is magnetic and impossible, loving and controlling, a man who has never had to ask for anything. Ditte is caught between duty and the future she was about to step into. Pernille Fischer Christensen films it all with great closeness and very little sentiment. No one makes speeches. People avoid the subject at dinner, and the avoidance says more than any confrontation could.

Jesper Christensen is extraordinary as Rikard: you believe both the charm and the tyranny, often in the same scene. Fischer Christensen had already won a Silver Bear at Berlin with her debut, A Soap, and she brought A Family back to the Berlinale competition. It is a film many people will recognise from their own lives, which is exactly why it hurts. Quiet, honest, and devastating.

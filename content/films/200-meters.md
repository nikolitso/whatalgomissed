Mustafa lives in a West Bank village. His wife Salwa and their children live in a town on the other side of Israel's separation wall, 200 meters away. At night the couple flash lights at each other across the concrete. It is a tender image and a cruel one, and it tells you everything about the life this family has been forced to build.

Then his son is hurt in an accident. Mustafa rushes to the checkpoint and is turned back on a technicality: his permit has not been renewed. So he pays smugglers to get him across, and a 200-meter walk becomes a 200-kilometer odyssey, crammed into a car with strangers, among them a German woman making a documentary. The road movie becomes a portrait of everything the wall does to people who have to live beside it: the waiting, the bribes, the humiliations, the small acts of solidarity.

This is Ameen Nayfeh's debut, drawn from his own family's experience, and it shows. Nothing is exaggerated because nothing needs to be. The absurdity is real. Ali Suliman anchors the film with a terrific central performance, a tired, proud man with a bad back and no patience left, and Lama Zreik is quietly strong as Salwa. It won the audience award at Venice Days in 2020, which makes sense: this is political cinema that never lectures, only shows. A small film about a very large injustice, told at human scale.

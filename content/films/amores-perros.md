A car crash in Mexico City connects three stories. Octavio, a young man from a poor neighbourhood, enters his dog Cofi in illegal dogfights to raise money, because he wants to run away with his brother's wife. Valeria, a beautiful model at the peak of her career, sees her life collapse after the accident. And El Chivo, a former guerrilla turned hit man who lives among stray dogs, is trying to find a way back to the daughter who thinks he is dead.

This was Alejandro González Iñárritu's debut, written by Guillermo Arriaga, and it arrived like a punch. The fractured structure, the handheld camera, the grainy, sweaty images of a city divided by class: it all feels urgent and alive. Dogs are everywhere, as a mirror of the people, as their victims, as their only loyal companions. Some scenes of the fights are hard to watch, but nothing is there for shock alone. Gael García Bernal became a star overnight, and Emilio Echevarría as El Chivo gives the film its sad, wounded soul.

It won the Grand Prix of the Critics' Week at Cannes, was nominated for the Oscar, and helped open the door for a new generation of Mexican filmmakers. Iñárritu and Arriaga would go on to 21 Grams and Babel, bigger and more polished, but never as raw. Ferocious, overflowing, a little too long, and still one of the great Latin American films.

Zain is around twelve, nobody knows exactly, because his parents never registered his birth. When we meet him he is in juvenile detention in Beirut, and he is suing his parents for bringing him into the world. The rest of the film shows us how he got there: a crowded flat in the slums, a younger sister sold into marriage, running away, and a stretch of days looking after Yonas, the baby son of an Ethiopian migrant worker, Rahil, who cannot afford to be found by the authorities.

Nadine Labaki shoots entirely on location with non-professional actors drawn from lives close to their characters. Zain Al Rafeea, who plays Zain, was himself a Syrian refugee child working in Beirut, and his performance is extraordinary: furious, funny, far too old for his face. Labaki plunges into poverty, statelessness and the lives of people the system has never seen, and she doesn't look away.

It isn't a flawless film. Labaki piles on misery, and the courtroom frame, where she herself plays Zain's lawyer, can feel contrived next to the street scenes. But the street scenes are so alive that the reservations fade. It won the Jury Prize at Cannes and an Oscar nomination for Best Foreign Language Film, making Labaki the first Arab woman director nominated in that category. Angry, overloaded and impossible to dismiss.

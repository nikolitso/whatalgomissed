Christoffer has built a life in Sweden with his Swedish partner, far from his family's industrial dynasty in Denmark. Then his father commits suicide, and his mother summons him back to take over the steelworks. He says yes, believing it will be temporary. It is not. The company is in trouble, the board wants blood, and the mother never stops pulling the strings.

This is the second part of Per Fly's trilogy about Danish class, after The Bench and before Manslaughter. Having started with the working class, Fly moves to the upper class with the same unflinching eye. There are no villains in the boardroom, only people doing what the family and the business demand. The film is about what duty costs and how much of yourself disappears in the process of becoming what others need you to be. Christoffer starts as a gentle, likable man. Watch the decisions he takes, one reasonable step at a time, and see who he becomes.

Ulrich Thomsen, the son who spoke up in Festen, is superb as Christoffer, a man whose face hardens almost imperceptibly from scene to scene. Ghita Nørby, one of the great ladies of Danish film, plays the mother with a terrifying calm. Fly shoots it all with cool restraint and lets the silences hurt. Quiet, devastating, and impeccably performed: Danish cinema at its most grown-up.

Jep Gambardella turns 65 on a Roman rooftop, in the middle of a party that never seems to end. Forty years ago he wrote one acclaimed novel. Since then he has written nothing but society journalism, and has become the king of the city's high life instead. Now he drifts through Rome's parties, ruins and ghosts, trying to understand why he never wrote a second book, and what exactly he has been doing with his life.

Sorrentino's Fellini-esque masterpiece is openly in conversation with La Dolce Vita, and it doesn't hide it. It is gorgeous, melancholic and quietly devastating beneath its surface glitter. The camera glides through palaces, convents and terraces at night, past aristocrats for hire, botox parties, a performance artist, a cardinal more interested in recipes than souls. It is a satire of Italian emptiness that is also a love letter to Rome, and Sorrentino never decides between the two. That is the point.

Toni Servillo is magnificent: elegant, cruel, tired, and every so often unexpectedly tender. The opening party sequence, with bodies dancing to a remix of Raffaella Carrà, is one of the great set pieces in 21st century Italian cinema. It is long and it wanders, and some will find it self-indulgent. I find it hypnotic. It won the Oscar for Best International Feature Film, along with the Golden Globe and the BAFTA. Watch it on the biggest screen you can find.

Budapest, 1956. Karcsi is the cocky star of the Hungarian water polo team, preparing for the Melbourne Olympics, and he has little time for politics. Then he meets Viki, a student at the heart of the protests, and the Hungarian Revolution breaks out around them. For a few days it looks as though the Soviets might actually leave. Then the tanks come back.

Krisztina Goda tells two stories in parallel: the uprising in the streets of Budapest and the team's journey to Australia, where they discover they will face the USSR in the tournament. The street fighting is staged on a big scale, with real anger in it, and the love story gives the politics a human face. It is not a subtle film. It is built like a big, old-fashioned epic, with swelling music and clear heroes. But it earns its emotion, because the history it tells is so extraordinary.

The match itself, played weeks after the revolution was crushed, became known as the Blood in the Water game, and it is one of the most famous in Olympic history. Goda stages it as a release of everything the film has built up. For the players, the pool is the only place left where Hungarians can fight back. History at its most visceral: sport as the only battlefield left open. Made for the fiftieth anniversary, and it still lands.

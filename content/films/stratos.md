Stratos is an ex-con who works nights in a bread factory and days as a hitman. He lives frugally, says almost nothing and saves every euro for one purpose: to pay for the escape of the friend he owes everything to, still serving time inside. Around him, a whole neighbourhood of small crooks and desperate families pull at him for favours. Then he learns his loyalty has been exploited.

Economides' Berlin Competition entry is quieter and more controlled than anything he made before. The shouting of Matchbox and Soul Kicking is still there in the people around Stratos, but the man himself is silence. The camera holds still, the nights are long and cold, and when the violence comes it lands harder for all the waiting. It is closer to Melville than anything Greek cinema had produced before, a crime film about a code of honour that nobody else around him still believes in.

The central performance by Vangelis Mourikis is extraordinary: a man who has reduced himself to function, discovering too late that he had something worth protecting. With his heavy face and tired eyes he barely needs dialogue. The film is long and slow, and it asks for patience. But it is Economides' most mature work, and it won a string of Hellenic Film Academy Awards for good reason. A bleak, powerful portrait of a Greece where loyalty is the only thing left that can't be bought, and even that is for sale.

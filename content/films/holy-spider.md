Mashhad, 2001. In Iran's holiest city, a man on a motorbike is picking up sex workers, taking them home and strangling them. Sixteen women die. The killer, Saeed Hanaei, is a construction worker and family man who believes he is cleansing the streets of sin, and a large part of the public agrees with him. A journalist from Tehran, Rahimi, arrives to investigate, and finds police who don't care, men who don't want her there, and a city that seems to be shrugging.

Ali Abbasi, the Iranian-Danish director of Border, was refused permission to film in Iran and shot in Jordan instead. He constructs a chilling procedural that is really an indictment of a society's moral logic. Unusually, we see the killer at home and at work from the start, so there is no mystery about who he is. The question is why nobody stops him, and what happens when someone finally tries.

Zar Amir Ebrahimi plays Rahimi, a fictional character, and won Best Actress at Cannes. The film is not perfect: some scenes are stilted, and the violence is shown more explicitly than some will want to see. But the second half, when the case reaches court and the public takes sides, hits very hard. The horror is not the killer. The horror is how many people agreed with him.

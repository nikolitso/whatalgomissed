Halla is around fifty, conducts a choir in a small Icelandic town and is loved by everyone. She is also the "Mountain Woman", a one-person resistance movement waging war against the aluminium industry. With a bow and arrow she sabotages power lines across the highlands, then hides from helicopters and drones in the moss and the rocks, living a double life that the whole country is talking about. Then a letter arrives telling her that her long-forgotten application to adopt a little girl from Ukraine has been approved, and everything gets more complicated.

Playful, political and completely committed to the idea that having right on your side is enough to keep going. Halldóra Geirharðsdóttir is magnificent as Halla, and also plays her twin sister Ása, a yoga teacher with very different ideas about how to change the world. The landscapes are vast and wild, and Erlingsson, who made Of Horses and Men, films them with obvious love.

The on-screen band playing the score is one of the most inspired formal choices in recent Nordic cinema. A trio of musicians, and later a group of Ukrainian singers in traditional dress, simply appear inside the scenes, standing in the middle of a field or a living room while Halla goes about her business. It is funny and strangely moving, like a Greek chorus with a tuba. The film premiered at Cannes, won the European Parliament's LUX Prize, and swept the Edda Awards at home. An eco-thriller with a heart, and one of the most original films of its year.

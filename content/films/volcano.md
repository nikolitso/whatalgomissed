Hannes is retiring from his job as a school caretaker in Reykjavík. He doesn't want a party, he doesn't want to talk, and he doesn't want anyone's affection, least of all from his grown-up children, who barely tolerate him. He grew up on the Westman Islands, and the film opens with old footage of the 1973 eruption that forced the whole island to flee. Something in him seems to have stayed frozen ever since. Then his wife Anna has a stroke, and the man who has kept everyone at arm's length decides that he alone will look after her.

A film about late-life reckoning: minimal dialogue, maximum weight. He has been cold and distant his whole life. The film watches him learn, very late, what it means to actually be present for another person. Washing her, feeding her, reading to her, lifting her. The small physical acts of care become the language he never managed to speak.

Theodór Júlíusson's central performance achieves something rare: you see a man changing in real time, without a word of explanation. Rúnar Rúnarsson, whose short film The Last Farm was nominated for an Oscar, made this as his first feature, and it premiered at Cannes. The film is grey and spare like the city it is set in, but never cold. Hard to watch in places, and very honest about old age, illness and love. A debut of real maturity.

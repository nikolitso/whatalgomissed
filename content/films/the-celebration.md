A father's 60th birthday party. A grand country hotel, a family dinner, relatives and old friends in their best clothes. Helge, the patriarch, is the man of the hour. His eldest son Christian, a quiet restaurateur who lives in Paris, has a speech prepared. His sister Helene and his volatile brother Michael are there too, and the absence of a fourth sibling hangs over everything. When Christian stands up and taps his glass, the evening goes somewhere nobody at the table was ready for.

This was the first Dogme 95 film, made under the rules Vinterberg wrote with Lars von Trier, and it is still the most devastating. It is a chamber drama shot on a small handheld camera by Anthony Dod Mantle, with no added music and no artificial light, that strips away every cinematic comfort and leaves something that feels like actual bearing witness. Vinterberg constructed the trap with perfect patience: the social rituals, the polite conversations, the formal dinner, all of it building pressure beneath a surface that eventually cracks open. Ulrich Thomsen, Henning Moritzen, Thomas Bo Larsen and Paprika Steen are all extraordinary.

What shocks most is not the revelation but the reaction: how a room full of respectable people would rather carry on dancing than face the truth. It won the Jury Prize at Cannes in 1998 and made Vinterberg's name at the age of 29. More than twenty-five years later, it still shocks. The film that changed Danish cinema.

Slimane is in his sixties, a Tunisian migrant who has spent most of his life in the shipyards of Sète, on France's Mediterranean coast. When he is laid off, he decides to spend his severance on a dream: a couscous restaurant on an old boat in the harbour, serving fish couscous made by his ex-wife. His large, chaotic, loving family has opinions about this. So does Rym, the daughter of the woman he now lives with, who becomes his fiercest ally.

Slow, generous and completely alive. Kechiche fills every frame with noise and warmth: a Sunday lunch where everyone talks over each other, plates passing, arguments about nothing. The camera stays so close that you feel like a guest at the table. Then he finds something devastating at the centre of it all, a quiet man who has given everything and is treated as if he were in the way. Habib Boufares, a non-professional, plays Slimane almost without words, and Hafsia Herzi is extraordinary as Rym.

The final sequence, which runs for over twenty minutes in what feels like real time, is one of the great set pieces in French cinema: a dinner, a delay, a dance, all the tension of a thriller built from a pot of couscous. The film won four Césars including Best Film and Best Director, and Herzi was named most promising actress. Kechiche went on to make Blue Is the Warmest Colour, but this is the film where his method works best. Long, yes. Worth every minute.

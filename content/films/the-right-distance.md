Mara arrives as a substitute teacher in a small town in the Po delta, the flat, foggy country between the river and the sea. She is young, pretty and curious, and the town watches her the way small towns watch any newcomer. Giovanni, a teenager who dreams of being a journalist, watches her too, and the local editor who gives him his first stories tells him the golden rule: keep the right distance. Then there is Hassan, a Tunisian mechanic who has lived there for years and is still, in everyone's eyes, the foreigner.

Keeping a proper distance turns out to be harder than any manual suggests: from people you like, from the community's tensions, from the immigrant workers at the edges of town. When something terrible happens, the town already knows who it wants to blame, and Mazzacurati shows how quickly gossip hardens into certainty.

This is a quiet film about proximity and prejudice in a part of Italy that rarely appears in Italian cinema. No Roman terraces, no Sicilian sun. Just bars, petrol stations, a riverbank and people who have known each other too long. Mazzacurati, who spent his career filming his native Veneto with affection and unease, never raises his voice. The tension builds in small scenes and long looks, and the ending is more troubling for being so calm. Not a famous film, but it should be.

The Alexandria Ceremonial Police Orchestra, a small group of Egyptian musicians in sky-blue uniforms, arrives in Israel to play at the opening of an Arab cultural centre. Nobody is at the airport to meet them. A mix-up over the name of a town sends them by bus to a small desert settlement with no hotel, no culture and no connection to anywhere. Dina, who runs the local café, takes pity on them, and the band is split up among the locals for one long night.

Eran Kolirin's debut is a quietly extraordinary film about stillness, loneliness, and the unexpected warmth that forms between people who have no common language. They speak in broken English, and the gaps between words do most of the work. Sasson Gabai is wonderful as Tawfiq, the stiff, proud conductor carrying an old sadness, and the late Ronit Elkabetz is magnetic as Dina, bored, funny and hungry for something to happen. A young Saleh Bakri plays the band's charmer, who teaches a shy local boy how to talk to a girl in one of the film's best scenes.

It won a prize in Un Certain Regard at Cannes and swept the Israeli Academy Awards, and years later it became a Tony-winning Broadway musical. The deadpan comedy recalls Kaurismäki, but the tenderness is Kolirin's own. It also says more about Arabs and Israelis than many films that try much harder, simply by putting them at the same table. Nothing happens. Everything happens.

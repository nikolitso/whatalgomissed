Thomas is a quiet German baker in Berlin. Oren is a married Israeli man who travels there for work and comes into Thomas's café for the Black Forest cake. They begin an affair. Then Oren stops calling, and Thomas learns that he has died in an accident back home. With nowhere to put his grief, Thomas travels to Jerusalem and finds the café run by Oren's widow, Anat. He takes a job there without telling her who he is.

Ofir Raul Graizer's debut is a film of small gestures and long silences. Tim Kalkhof plays Thomas as a man of almost no words who speaks through baking, and Sarah Adler is superb as Anat, a woman trying to keep her café and her son afloat while carrying a grief she can't fully share. The film also touches, lightly, on very Israeli questions: whether a German's cakes can be kosher, who belongs in a family business, what religion demands of grief. Graizer never underlines anything. He trusts the faces and the food.

It won seven Israeli Academy Awards and was Israel's submission to the Oscars. Some may find it too slow, but I found the slowness was the point: grief doesn't hurry, and neither does the film. It is a film about grief, love across every kind of border, and the strange tenderness that sometimes grows between strangers. Gentle, sad and beautifully made. Don't watch it hungry.

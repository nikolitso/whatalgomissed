A middle-aged Danish man has lived his entire life in the closet. He is married, respected, settled, and deeply buried under the version of himself he built decades ago to make life simpler. Nobody suspects anything, possibly not even him on his better days. Then something cracks, and he finds himself at a crossroads between the comfortable lie and something that finally resembles truth.

Jesper W. Nielsen, a veteran of Danish film and television, handles this with tact. There are no grand coming-out speeches and no villains. The film is interested in something subtler and sadder: the specific cost of self-concealment across a lifetime. The energy spent maintaining the performance. The people damaged by the distance, especially the ones who loved him most and never knew why something was missing. And the self, slowly disappeared, until a man looks at his own life as if it belonged to someone else.

What I liked most is that it doesn't pretend the truth arrives free. Coming out at that age is not a liberation in one scene. It is also a kind of demolition, and others have to live in the rubble. The film lets everyone keep their dignity, including the wife, which is rarer than it should be. Not a loud film, and not a perfect one, but an honest and humane one, and the kind of story Danish cinema tells better than most.

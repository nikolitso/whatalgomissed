Piero grows up in Ovosodo, a working-class neighbourhood of Livorno, with a father in and out of prison, a dead mother and a disabled brother. He is bright but passive, the kind of boy things happen to. A teacher sees something in him and pushes him toward the classical lyceum and the books. There he meets Tommaso, a rich, rebellious boy who drags him into a different life. And Piero has to work out who he is: the boy from the neighbourhood or the one who got away.

Virzì's breakthrough film is funny, restless and completely alive. He tells it through Piero's voice-over, dry and self-mocking, and fills every corner of the frame with Livorno: the port, the factories, the families shouting from balconies, the left-wing politics that everyone breathes without thinking about. Edoardo Gabbriellini, in his debut, is perfect as Piero, and Nicoletta Braschi is warm as the teacher who changes his life.

What makes it special is its lack of sentimentality. Growing up here means compromise, small disappointments and a few unexpected joys, and Virzì finds comedy in all of it without losing the sadness underneath. It won the Special Grand Jury Prize at Venice and announced him as one of the defining voices of his generation, the director who would go on to make Caterpillar, Human Capital and Like Crazy. One of the great Italian coming-of-age films, and almost entirely unknown outside Italy.

Asia had her daughter Vika very young, after emigrating from Russia to Israel, and the two have grown up almost side by side. Asia is a nurse in Jerusalem, brash and restless, who still goes out at night, has an affair at work, and has always kept an emotional distance from her teenage daughter. Vika has a degenerative disease. When her health suddenly deteriorates, the roles begin to reverse, and Asia must become the mother she never quite managed to be.

Ruthy Pribar's debut is quiet and precise. There are no big speeches and no hospital melodrama. Instead there are small gestures, long silences and jokes that land wrong. Vika, like any teenager, wants to live before it's too late, including experiencing sex for the first time, and Asia's response pushes the film into difficult, morally ambiguous territory. Pribar handles it with great tact.

Alena Yiv is excellent as Asia, tough on the surface and frightened underneath. Shira Haas, who won Best Actress at Tribeca, is extraordinary as Vika, a fragile body with a fierce spirit. The film swept the Israeli Academy Awards with nine wins. It is unsentimental almost to the end, and that is why the final act carries such almost unbearable tenderness. A small film about a mother and daughter, and one of the best Israeli films of recent years.

Gianni is a young man who abandoned his son at birth, when the boy's mother died and the baby was born severely disabled. Fifteen years later, the relatives who raised Paolo ask Gianni to take him to Berlin for treatment at a specialist clinic. So a father meets his teenage son for the first time, on a journey neither of them chose.

Gianni Amelio, the director of Stolen Children and Lamerica, adapts a novel by Giuseppe Pontiggia and refuses sentiment at every turn. The relationship that develops is uncomfortable, clumsy, and ultimately more generous than most films that try to be kind. Gianni is unprepared, guilty, sometimes irritated, sometimes proud. Paolo is funny, stubborn and demanding. In the clinic Gianni meets Nicole, a French woman who has spent twenty years caring for her own disabled daughter, and her honesty about the exhaustion and the love is the hardest thing in the film to watch.

Kim Rossi Stuart is excellent as the father, all good intentions and panic, and Charlotte Rampling is extraordinary in a handful of scenes. But the film belongs to Andrea Rossi, who plays Paolo with remarkable openness. Amelio shoots it with a documentary plainness: hospital corridors, trains, hotel rooms, a long drive north. It competed at Venice and deserved more attention than it got. A film that trusts you to bring your own feelings to it rather than telling you what to feel.

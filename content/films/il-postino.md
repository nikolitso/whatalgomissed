Early 1950s. The Chilean poet Pablo Neruda, exiled for his politics, arrives on a small island off the coast of Italy. Mario, the shy, unemployed son of a fisherman, gets a job as the poet's personal postman, delivering bags of letters from admirers around the world. Slowly, a friendship forms. Mario asks about metaphors, discovers poetry, and uses it to court Beatrice, the most beautiful woman on the island, which her aunt does not appreciate at all.

Michael Radford, adapting a novel by Antonio Skármeta, keeps everything simple: the light on the sea, the empty village squares, the long conversations on the beach. It is warm, unhurried and quietly devastating. Philippe Noiret is a generous, worldly Neruda, and the music by Luis Bacalov, which won the Oscar, is part of the film's soul. Underneath the charm there is a real idea: that poetry belongs to whoever needs it, and that it can change how a man sees his own small world.

What makes it almost unbearable to watch today is Massimo Troisi. He co-wrote the film and plays Mario with enormous tenderness. He postponed heart surgery to finish it, and died the day after filming wrapped. You can see how ill he is in some scenes, and it adds a sadness the film never asks for. Nominated for five Oscars, including Best Picture and Best Actor. A gentle classic, and a farewell.

A fourteen-year-old girl tells the school psychologist that her father has sexually abused her. The father is shocked, angry, and says she is a chronic liar who has always invented things. The mother doesn't know whom to believe. He is arrested. Is he guilty? Henrik Ruben Genz builds his film on that question and then refuses, deliberately and strategically, to give you a clean answer.

Troels Lyby plays the father, and he is very good at making us doubt our own sympathies. One moment he seems a decent man trapped in a nightmare. The next, a detail lands and the floor tilts again. Sofie Gråbøl is excellent as the mother, slowly crushed between her husband and her daughter. Genz, who later made the much darker comedy Terribly Happy, constructs the ambiguity with care, making sure you understand each version of events, and then refuses to adjudicate.

That refusal is the film's strength and its limit. It is about accusation, evidence and belief, and about the machinery that takes over once a claim enters the system and can no longer be easily taken back: police, lawyers, social services, neighbours. But at times the ambiguity feels more like a design than a discovery, and the characters around the family are thin. It inevitably invites comparison with The Hunt, which came later and went deeper. Still, a serious, uncomfortable film that asks the right questions and trusts you to sit with them.

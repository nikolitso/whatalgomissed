Amal teaches literature at a secondary school in Brussels, and she believes her classroom should be a place where her students can say anything. When Monia, a teenage girl in her class, is bullied because she is a lesbian, Amal takes her side. Then she decides to teach the poems of Abu Nuwas, the great classical Arabic poet who wrote openly about wine and desire. Some students refuse to listen. Then their parents get involved. What starts as an argument in a classroom grows into a campaign of harassment, and Amal finds herself more and more alone.

Jawad Rhalib builds the film like a thriller, with the tension rising scene by scene, and it never lets you relax. The school is shown in all its complexity: a headteacher who wants above all to avoid trouble, a religion teacher whose influence over the students reaches further than anyone admits, and teenagers caught between their families, their faith and their own feelings. Lubna Azabal is extraordinary as Amal, stubborn, warm and exhausted, a woman who refuses to back down even as the cost keeps rising. She won the Magritte Award for Best Actress, and the young cast is just as convincing.

It is a film about freedom of thought, about the courage it takes to be a teacher, and about how quickly fear can spread through a community when nobody wants to speak. It is hard and uncomfortable, and some will find it too direct. But it is made with real urgency and real compassion, especially for the students. Powerful, important and gripping. A must see.

On the margins of Tokyo, a makeshift family lives packed into a tiny house. Osamu and the boy Shota steal from supermarkets with rehearsed routines. Nobuyo works at a laundry, her younger "sister" Aki works at a peep-show club, and Grandma's pension keeps the roof over their heads. One winter night Osamu and Shota find a little girl, Yuri, shivering alone on a balcony, and take her home. Nobody comes looking for her.

Koreeda asks one question and spends two hours refusing to answer it simply: what actually makes a family? He has been asking it for years, in Nobody Knows and Like Father, Like Son, but never this richly. The answer the film arrives at is uncomfortable and very moving. He never forces a tear. He just shows you people living, eating noodles, going to the beach, teaching a child to steal, and lets the weight accumulate on its own. Lily Franky, Sakura Ando and the great Kirin Kiki are wonderful, and the gentle score by Haruomi Hosono keeps everything soft until it can't be.

When the truth about this "family" starts to come out, the film changes key, and Ando has a scene in an interview room that is among the finest acting of the decade. It won the Palme d'Or at Cannes and an Oscar nomination. A beautiful, humane film that rewards every bit of patience you give it.

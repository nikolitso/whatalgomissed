Emad and Rana, a Tehran couple, are rehearsing Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman with an amateur theatre company when their apartment building starts to crack and they have to get out in the middle of the night. A friend from the cast finds them a new flat. The previous tenant, a woman the neighbours speak about in low voices, has left her things behind. One evening Rana buzzes the door open, thinking it is her husband, and goes to take a shower. Someone else comes in.

What the film is really about is how one man's damaged sense of honour drives him toward something that will destroy more than it repairs. Rana wants to forget. Emad, played by Shahab Hosseini with a slow, simmering pride, wants to find the man, and he won't go to the police. Farhadi uses Miller's play as a mirror. Willy Loman's delusions echo Emad's, and the tragedy is the same: a man protecting an image of himself that was never quite true.

Hosseini won Best Actor at Cannes, and Farhadi took Best Screenplay. The film then won the Oscar, Farhadi's second after A Separation, though he refused to attend the ceremony in protest at the American travel ban. It isn't quite at that earlier film's level. But the final half hour, in a half-empty building, is as tense and morally knotted as anything he has made.

Daphne is a teenage judo athlete from a remote Greek island. She is talented, stubborn and a little lost, and when her sensei leaves for Athens she follows him, chasing an Olympic dream that nobody back home quite believes in. He has been absent from competitive judo for years and is searching for something he can't name. In the big city, both of them have to start again, in gyms, rented rooms and the long hours between training sessions.

Nikos Pastras makes a film that is part sports drama, part coming-of-age story, and entirely its own thing. There are bouts and weigh-ins and tournaments, but he isn't interested in the usual underdog formula. He is interested in bodies, in discipline, in the strange intimacy between an athlete and the person who trains her. The film is quiet and assured, shot with patience, and it trusts its actors to carry scenes without explaining them.

The relationship between Daphne and her coach earns its emotional weight without manipulation. Neither of them is simply a mentor or a pupil; they need each other in ways that change as the film goes on, and it is genuinely moving in its final act. It was selected for Berlin in 2025 and won Best Greek Film at Thessaloniki. One of the best Greek films of recent years, and the one that surprised me most. Greek cinema beyond the Weird Wave, and in very good shape.

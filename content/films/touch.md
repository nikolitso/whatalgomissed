Kristófer is an elderly widower in Reykjavík. The pandemic is about to close the world, his doctor has given him news he doesn't want to hear, and he decides that before his memory goes, he has to find out what happened to a woman he lost half a century ago. In late-sixties London he was a young student who dropped out and took a job washing dishes in a small Japanese restaurant. There he met Miko, the owner's daughter. One day the family vanished without a word.

Kormákur, adapting Ólafur Jóhann Ólafsson's novel, moves between past and present with rare delicacy, letting both timelines breathe. The old man travels through an emptying London and on to Japan. The young one learns to cook, learns some Japanese, and falls in love slowly and seriously. The director's son Pálmi Kormákur plays the young Kristófer, and the singer Egill Ólafsson is wonderful as the old one: gentle, dignified, a man trying to finish a sentence he started fifty years ago. Behind the love story is a painful piece of history, and the film handles it with respect.

Kormákur has spent years making Hollywood action films like Everest and Beast, and it is a surprise to see him make something this quiet and personal. Deeply felt, quietly beautiful, and honest about what we carry through a lifetime and what we finally let go. One of the most underrated European films of 2023.

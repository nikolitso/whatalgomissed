Marta graduates in philosophy with top marks and a thesis on Hannah Arendt. In Italy, that and a few euros will buy you a coffee. The only work she can find is at a call centre selling water purifiers by phone, staffed by young women who start every morning with a chanted, dancing team ritual. Meanwhile she babysits for a single mother to make ends meet. The irony writes itself, and Virzì lets it.

Paolo Virzì, the director of Ovosodo and later Human Capital, turns economic satire into something warm and sharply observed. The call centre becomes a microcosm of precarious employment: the cheerful cruelty of performance targets, the public humiliation of whoever sold least that week, the managers who talk like motivational posters. Sabrina Ferilli is very funny and a little tragic as the team leader who believes in the system because she has nothing else. Isabella Ragonese plays Marta with a dry intelligence that keeps the film from becoming a cartoon. She watches this world the way a philosopher should, and slowly realises she is part of it.

It is a comedy, but the laughs leave a mark. Virzì understands the specific modern misery of being overqualified for the only job on offer, a whole generation told that education would save them. Few Italian films of the 2000s caught the mood of the country's young as well as this one. Funny, angry, and more relevant now than when it came out.
